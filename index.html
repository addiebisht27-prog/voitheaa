Physics · 4 min read
How GPS uses Einstein's relativity
Your phone knows where you are to within a few meters. But without Einstein, it would be off by miles.

Every time you open Google Maps, a network of 30+ satellites orbiting 20,000 km above Earth is quietly doing some serious physics. GPS works by measuring how long it takes a signal to travel from a satellite to your phone — and since signals travel at the speed of light, tiny time differences translate to precise distances.

But here's the problem: time doesn't tick at the same rate everywhere. Einstein's theory of relativity tells us two things that mess with GPS clocks. First, special relativity says that clocks moving fast tick slower than clocks standing still. The satellites are moving at about 14,000 km/h, so their clocks run slow by about 7 microseconds per day.

Second, general relativity says that clocks in weaker gravity tick faster. Up at 20,000 km altitude, gravity is weaker, so the satellite clocks run fast by about 45 microseconds per day. Combined, the clocks gain roughly 38 microseconds per day compared to clocks on the ground.

That sounds tiny — but at the speed of light, 38 microseconds of error adds up to about 11 kilometers of positional error per day. Without relativistic corrections, your GPS would be useless within hours. The engineers who built GPS had to solve Einstein's equations just to make your phone's map work.

Next time you use navigation, remember: you're benefiting from one of the most elegant ideas in the history of science.

The math behind Spotify's shuffle algorithm
Why "truly random" shuffle felt wrong — and how Spotify made it feel right.

In 2014, Spotify's engineering team published a surprising blog post: they had intentionally made their shuffle algorithm less random. Users were complaining that shuffle felt broken — the same artists kept coming up back to back. But when engineers looked at the data, the shuffle was perfectly random. So what was going on?

The answer is a classic problem in probability: humans are terrible at perceiving randomness. In a truly random sequence, clustering is expected. If you flip a coin 100 times, you'll almost certainly see streaks of 5 or 6 heads in a row. That's not a glitch — it's math. But to our brains, it feels like something is wrong.

Spotify's original algorithm was a Fisher-Yates shuffle — a classic computer science technique that generates a perfectly uniform random permutation of a list. Every ordering is equally likely. Mathematically flawless. But psychologically awful.

Their new algorithm, inspired by research from Martin Fischler, spreads artists evenly across the queue. If you have 3 songs by the same artist in a 30-song playlist, the algorithm places them roughly 10 songs apart — guaranteeing they won't cluster. This is less statistically random, but it feels more random to the listener.

The lesson? Sometimes the goal isn't to be mathematically correct — it's to be humanly correct. Understanding how people perceive patterns is just as important as understanding the patterns themselves.

Combinatorics · 3 min read
Why chess has more moves than atoms in the universe
A 8×8 board. 32 pieces. And more possible games than you can possibly imagine.

There are an estimated 1080 atoms in the observable universe. That's a 1 followed by 80 zeros — an incomprehensibly large number. And yet, the number of possible chess games dwarfs it entirely.

The number of legal chess positions is estimated at around 1044. The number of possible games — sequences of moves from start to finish — is estimated at 10120. This is known as the Shannon number, named after Claude Shannon, the father of information theory.

Why so many? It comes down to combinatorics — the mathematics of counting possibilities. After just the first move, White has 20 possible moves and Black has 20 responses: 400 possible positions. By move 3, there are over 9 million possible game states. The number explodes exponentially with every move.

This is why chess computers can't simply "look up" the right move — there are too many possibilities to store. Instead, they use algorithms like minimax and alpha-beta pruning to intelligently prune the search tree, evaluating only the most promising branches.

Chess is a perfect example of how simple rules — just 6 piece types on a 64-square board — can generate infinite complexity. It's the same reason math and science feel hard at first: the building blocks are simple, but the combinations are endless. That's not a reason to give up. That's what makes it beautiful.
